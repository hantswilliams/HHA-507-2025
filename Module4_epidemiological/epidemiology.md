# Epidemiology
Epidemiology is the study of the determinants, distribution, and frequency of disease in human populations

Who gets disease and why

Epidemiologists study sick and well people to determine the crucial difference between those who get disease and those who are spared

# Purpose of Epidemiology
To provide a basis for developing disease control and prevention measures for groups at risk.  This translates into developing public health measures to prevent or control disease.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# Population Focus
The focus of epidemiology is on the occurrence of health and disease in the population. What happens to many

The population approach contrasts with clinical medicine’s primary concern with health and disease in the individual.  What happens to one

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
# What is a Population
The common definition of a population is “All the inhabitants of a given country or area considered together;…”

A “population” can also be groups of individuals that share a common thread
Clinical populations
Subgroups of the population by age, race, etc…

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# Epidemiology as a Population Science Approach
Diseases are expressed biologically in individuals, however, no epidemiological study can be done on one person

Epidemiology studies humans in the aggregate (i.e. groups)

Conclusions are directly applicable to the groups studied (Conclusions are only indirectly applicable to individuals)

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Descriptive and Inferential
Descriptive Statistics deal with the enumeration, organization and graphical representation of data from a sample

Inferential Statistics deal with reaching conclusions from incomplete information, that is, generalizing from the specific sample.
use available information in a sample to draw inferences about the population from which the sample was selected

# Broad Characterization of Epidemiological Practices
1. Descriptive Epidemiology
– Examining, identifying, and reporting on the frequency and distribution of disease in a population.  Learning the basic features of its distribution.

2. Analytic Epidemiology
– Identifying factors underlying disease or health events.  Testing a hypothesis by studying how exposures relate to outcomes

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
# Broad Characterization of Epidemiological Practices
3. Intervention Development to Reduce Disease Burden
– Using information from analytical studies, develop strategies centered around an important exposure factor.  Test these strategies with clinical trials.

4. Program Evaluation
– Looking at effectiveness of programs for disease control in the community

# Describing diseases
Endemic: constant presence and/or usual prevalence of a disease or infectious agent in a population within a geographic area

Epidemic (outbreak): disease occurrence among a population that is in excess of what is expected in a given time and place

Pandemic: refers to an epidemic that has spread over several countries or continents, usually affecting a large number of people.

# Formulating the Research Question
Define Dependent Variable (DV): outcome of interest (e.g., disease occurrence)

Define Independent Variable (IV): exposure/risk factor (e.g., smoking status)

Hypothesis example: 'Smokers (IV) [exposure] have higher incidence of lung cancer (DV) [disease]

Set up formulas to quantify association between IV and DV

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# Measures of Disease Frequency
Incidence = new cases / population at risk (over time)

Prevalence = existing cases / total population

Example: COVID-19 14-day incidence

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Examples
Incidence: In a study of 1,000 people, 50 developed influenza in 2024 → Incidence = 50/1000 = 5%

Prevalence: In 2024, 25m of 350m people have diabetes → Prevalence = 25m/350m = 7.14%

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
# Measures of Association
Relative Risk (RR) = Incidence in exposed / Incidence in unexposed

Odds Ratio (OR) = (odds of exposure in cases) / (odds of exposure in controls)

Applications in cohort vs case-control studies

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
# Examples
Relative Risk:
Lung cancer incidence among smokers = 20/100;
Lung cancer incidence among nonsmokers = 2/100

→ RR = (0.20/0.02) = 10

Interpretation:
the risk for smokers (20%) is 10 times greater than the risk of non smokers (2%)

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
# Examples
Odds Ratio (OR):
(Odds of outcome occurring in exposed) / (Odds of outcome occurring in unexposed group or control)

OR = 1 no association
OR > 1 increased odds of exposure
OR < 1 decreased odds of exposure

A - count of exposed and have disease
B - count of exposed and do not have disease
C - count of unexposed and have disease
D - count of unexposed and do not have disease
